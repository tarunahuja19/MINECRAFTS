Reading this dashboard

R4 watches one longwall panel with 31 ground sensors and a classical (non-AI) detector. This page explains what the markers, zones and alarms mean, and answers the two questions people ask first: why does one event light up so much of the map, and why does it raise dozens of alarms instead of five or six.

1 · The nodes

Every node is the same hardware — an ESP32, a LoRa radio, and seven cheap sensors in a box on a concrete peg. What differs is where it sits and what job that gives it. On the map, shape = role and colour = live state.

NODE ROLES (SHAPE ON THE MAP)

Gateway1Outer ring, on the surface hut, 10 m mast Collects every packet, runs the detector, raises alarms, drives the siren and SMS, and buffers to SD if the backhaul drops. The one node you cannot move or rotate.

TIER (SPACING / SAMPLING DENSITY)

The Tier field on a node (1A, 1B, 1C, 2A, 2B, 3) records how densely that part of the field is sampled. Tier 1 nodes sit on the steep trough edges at 25 m spacing where curvature changes fastest; the flat interior is sampled coarsely at ~75 m. Tier 2 are the anchors, Tier 3 is the gateway. The spacing is derived from the Knothe influence radius (r ≈ 75–197 m depending on depth), not chosen by eye.

2 · The zones and the map overlays

The simulation window is split into a 6×6 grid of 36 zones, each 100 × 100 m. A zone is not a sensor — it is a patch of ground whose truth state (from the physics model) is tracked independently. Zones drive the SURFACE shading on the map and are the unit each alarm is raised against.

ZONE STATES — THE GROUND-TRUTH LADDER
StateAlarm levelTrigger (truth)Meaning
STABLE—S < 0.05 mNo measurable movement. Never raises an alarm.
SETTLING—S ≥ 0.05 m, strain lowUniform sinking, no dangerous stretch yet. Normal operation over an active panel. Never raises an alarm.
TENSION1ε ≥ 4.0 mm/m or κ ≥ 0.9×10⁻⁴/mGround is being stretched toward its limit. Fissures becoming likely.
CRITICAL2ε ≥ 5.3 mm/m or κ ≥ 1.5×10⁻⁴/mTensile/compressive limit exceeded. Fissure formation predicted.
FAILED3Step drop ΔS ≥ 0.30 m or κ ≥ 4.0×10⁻⁴/mSudden pillar breach / void collapse in that patch.

A zone can only ever move up this ladder, never down. Subsidence is irreversible on this timescale, so once a zone reaches CRITICAL it stays at least CRITICAL until the simulation is rewound. This latch was added deliberately — without it, a zone sitting near a threshold flickered CRITICAL → FAILED → CRITICAL with the truth-field noise and raised a fresh alarm on every bounce.

Gate T50 also guarantees a zone is always seen at CRITICAL before it is seen at FAILED — there is always a warning window, even for a sudden collapse.

3 · Why one event lights up so much of the map

This is expected, and it is physics, not a display bug. Three things stack:

  1. The subsidence bowl is wider than the whole window. The panel is 250 m wide and the Knothe influence zone reaches ~197 m past each edge, so the trough spans roughly 250 + 2×197 ≈ 640 m — wider than the 600 m simulation window. Once extraction is underway, the regional trough alone touches nearly every one of the 36 zones.
  2. Strain reaches much further than the dip you can see. Strain and curvature are the second derivative of the surface. A cave-in of radius 75 m produces a visible dip only ~150 m across, but its curvature field — with a wide negative outer lobe — pushes zones past the TENSION threshold well beyond that. The steepest, most-stretched ground is on the rim of the bowl, not at its centre.
  3. The event sits on top of an already-moving surface. The collapse delta is added to the growing regional bowl, so zones that were already SETTLING only need a small extra push to cross into TENSION or CRITICAL.

In the run behind the current screenshot, 26 zones (Z-07 through Z-36) crossed at least one threshold. For a panel-scale roof collapse that is the correct answer — a real one would crack the ground over a comparable area. If you want a local event, trigger a cave-in with a small radius and low severity, away from the panel centre.

4 · Why dozens of alarms and not five or six

The alarm count is not a repeat-firing bug. That bug existed — a zone used to re-alarm every 60 s — and it was fixed. What you see now is one alarm per zone, per severity level it enters:

  • A zone that goes TENSION → CRITICAL → FAILED raises three alarms (IDs ALM-Z-13-TENSION, ALM-Z-13-CRITICAL, ALM-Z-13-FAILED).
  • Each is raised once — the ID is keyed on zone + state, so the same transition can never be logged twice.
  • 26 zones × roughly 2–3 levels each ≈ the ~37 (and, accumulated across reruns, ~69) rows in the history.

This is deliberate. The detector's design rule is that every alarm names the exact zone and channel that caused it, so a mine safety officer can walk to that specific peg. Collapsing "Z-13 reached TENSION" and "Z-13 reached FAILED" into one line would throw away the escalation history that matters most.

Read the wall of alarms as one incident: sort by time, and the cluster of zones marching TENSION → CRITICAL → FAILED together is the collapse. The count is the resolution, not the severity.

HOW A CANDIDATE ANOMALY BECOMES AN ALARM

The gateway detector is deterministic — no neural network in the alarm path. Every cycle it runs, in order:

  1. Staleness gate. If the newest data is older than 3 cycles, emit STALE — not "all clear". A live radio with a dead backhaul must never look green.
  2. Quorum gate. Need ≥ 5 nodes with |z-score| > 3 on strain. One node cannot raise an alarm — a single lying sensor is structurally unable to.
  3. Bowl fit. Least-squares fit a Knothe bowl to the readings. R² < 0.85 ⇒ scattered noise, not ground ⇒ no alarm.
  4. Blast veto. Cross-check the DGMS blast register. A logged blast within 120 s and 500 m that decays fast is vetoed. An unlogged blast escalates instead.
  5. Machine veto. 50 Hz conveyor is notched out; 8–20 Hz truck is vetoed; 100–250 Hz is real rock and is kept.
  6. Silence rule. ≥ 3 nearby nodes going dead together is an event — unless they reappear on their backup radio slot (relay death), or a lightning strike is logged within 5 km.
  7. Persistence. The anomaly must hold ≥ 3 cycles — unless a node last-gasped or cracks are spreading.

5 · Replaying what happened ("run it back")

The 90-Day Replay Controller (left of the map, and full-size on the HISTORY tab) plays recorded telemetry and alarms back through the same panels the live feed uses.

  • FROM / TO set the window. Leave them blank, or widen them, if nothing plays — see the note below.
  • PLAY / STOP and 10× / 50× / 100× control playback. The map, node markers, alarm history and charts all update as the replay runs.
  • The NODE KILL & ALARM TIMELINE (HISTORY tab) shows which nodes dropped and when, against the alarm stream — the clearest single view of a past incident.
  • The PAST ALARM BROWSER and FALSE-POSITIVE SWEEP let you page through every recorded alarm and see which were later judged noise.
IF THE REPLAY SHOWS NOTHING

The simulation clock runs ahead of real time (10× by default, faster if you speed it up), so freshly recorded rows are timestamped in the sim's future. If you set TO to "today" the query finds nothing. Fix: clear both date fields, or widen the range well past today.
